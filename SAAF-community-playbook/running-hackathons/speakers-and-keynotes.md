# Speakers And Keynotes

Speakers exist to *frame the day*, not to fill it. The keynote slot
is small, the bar is high, and saying no is a core competence of the
captain.

This page covers how to invite, brief, and curate speakers — internal
and external — and how to handle the inevitable vendor pitch
requests.

---

## The Speaker Slots In The Default Day

The morning is the speaker block; the afternoon is the hackathon.
Speaker slots are concentrated in the morning, hard-capped, and end
before lunch.

| Slot | Length | Who | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Welcome (09:00) | 15 min | Captain | Frame the day, manifesto, practicalities |
| Keynote (09:15) | 30 min hard cap | One external or senior internal | One sharp idea that primes the room |
| Presentation 1 (10:00) | 30 min hard cap | Practitioner / case study | A working example the room can use |
| Presentation 2 (10:30) | 30 min hard cap | Different angle from Presentation 1 | Methodology, regulatory, or quality perspective |
| Plan walkthrough (11:15) | 45 min | Captain or track lead | Today's plans, tracks, claim moment |
| Demo session (16:00) | 45 min | Teams | *Not a speaker slot.* Teams demo. |
| Closing (16:45) | 15 min | Captain | Thanks, next session, manifesto line |

That's it. There is no closing keynote. There is no afternoon panel.
There are no "fireside chats." Resist these. They take build time
away.

The keynote and the two presentations together are roughly **90
minutes of speaker content** — enough to seed real ideas, short
enough that the room isn't exhausted before lunch. Three speakers
maximum. Four if one of them is a tight 20-minute slot.

For a regional chapter's first alignment session, the format is
different (see
[../regional-chapter/first-alignment-session.md](../regional-chapter/first-alignment-session.md)) —
but at hackathons themselves, this is the slot inventory.

## What A Good Keynote Looks Like

Thirty minutes. One sharp idea. The kind of thing that, two hours
into the afternoon build, a team remembers and applies.

Examples that worked at SAAF NL sessions:

- A working auditor demoing how they actually use AI in their day-to-
  day, including what failed.
- A Professional Practices lead explaining how their firm decided what an AI-
  generated finding has to clear before it is acceptable.
- An external researcher explaining one specific concept (prompt
  injection, agent debate, retrieval-augmented generation) at the
  level needed by the room.
- A senior auditor from a regulator explaining what they are looking
  for when they review AI-touched audit work.

Examples that don't work:

- Vendor "thought leadership" with no live demo.
- "The history of AI" or "where AI is going" — the room is past
  generic framing.
- "Lessons from my organisation's AI transformation" — too long,
  too narrative, not enough usable substance.
- Anything longer than thirty minutes.

## Invitation Process

Six weeks before the session: invite the keynote speaker. Earlier is
better; speakers worth having are booked weeks in advance.

The invitation email is short and explicit:

```
Subject: SAAF [Chapter Name] keynote — [Date]

Hi [Name],

I'm [Captain Name], captain of [Chapter Name]. We run a quarterly
hackathon for ~50 auditors, Professional Practices leads, analysts, and engineers
building AI audit agents together.

For our [Date] session at [Venue], we'd love a 30-minute keynote on
[specific topic — pick something they're actually known for].

The audience is hands-on builders, not executives. They want one
sharp idea, not a survey. Thirty minutes hard. The morning is talks;
the hackathon starts after lunch.

If you're interested, reply with availability. Happy to brief in
more detail. Travel and a small honorarium covered.

[Captain Name]
[Chapter Name]
```

Two paragraphs of context, one paragraph of ask, one paragraph of
logistics. If the speaker can't decide from this, the speaker is too
busy or the topic is wrong — both are signals to move on.

## Speaker Brief

Once the speaker has agreed, send a one-page brief two weeks before
the day. Use [appendix/templates/speaker-brief.md](../appendix/templates/speaker-brief.md).
The brief contains:

- The chapter's vision paragraph.
- The audience profile (roles, seniority mix, what they care about).
- The exact slot: time, length, format.
- What a good talk looks like (with one concrete example from a past
  session if possible).
- What a bad talk looks like (with one concrete example —
  often a vendor-pitch shape).
- The hard time limit and the cut-off signal.
- Logistics: arrival time, AV requirements, contact on the day.

The brief is a contract. Speakers who read it carefully give better
talks. Speakers who skim it give worse talks. The brief filters in
both directions.

## The Time Limit

Thirty minutes is thirty minutes. The captain is the timekeeper.

Tools:

- A visible timer on screen, started when the speaker begins.
- A signal at five minutes remaining (e.g., the captain stands at
  the back of the room with a hand raised).
- A signal at one minute remaining (raised hand, both hands).
- At zero, the captain walks to the front and gently introduces the
  next thing.

Speakers who run over by two minutes are forgiven. Speakers who run
over by ten are damaging the day — and the captain is the only
person who can stop them. Do.

If you let a speaker run over, every speaker after them treats the
time limit as advisory.

## Honorarium And Travel

For external speakers:

- **Travel and accommodation** are covered, fully and unbureaucratically.
- **A small honorarium** is appropriate for non-employees: enough to
  signal seriousness, not enough to turn the slot into a paid speaking
  gig. The exact figure varies by region; €250–€750 is a common
  range for a 30-minute slot.
- **No honorarium for senior people speaking on behalf of their
  employer** — they are doing it as part of their day job and on
  behalf of an organisation that already benefits from the
  community.

Don't pay sponsors to speak. Don't accept payment from a speaker for
a slot. Both arrangements break the chapter's neutrality.

## The Vendor Pitch Problem

Vendors will ask to speak. Some will be subtle ("we'd love to share
some lessons learned"). Some will be blunt ("we'll cover the catering
if our CTO speaks for thirty minutes"). Both happen, and the
captain's response shapes the chapter's identity.

The default answer: **vendors do not speak in the keynote slot.**

Acceptable vendor participation in a session:

- Vendors **attend** as participants and contribute to teams.
- Vendors **sponsor** catering or venue without speaker rights.
- Vendors **demo a working open-source artefact** in the demo
  session, on the same terms as any other team — five minutes, hard
  cap.
- Vendors **share research** on a non-product topic (agent
  evaluation methodology, model behaviour, audit-relevant benchmarks),
  *if* the talk would survive removal of all product mentions.

Not acceptable:

- A vendor's product demo as the keynote.
- A vendor's "thought leadership" talk that reduces to a product
  pitch.
- A vendor's logo on the keynote slide as part of the slot's
  branding.

How to say no:

```
Thanks for the offer. We have a strict format for the keynote slot —
thirty minutes, one sharp idea, no product framing. That isn't going
to work for what you're proposing.

We'd love to have you and your team at the session as participants —
join a build team, claim a plan, contribute to the chapter. The
demo session at the end of the day is open if you have a working
open-source artefact you'd like to show. Five minutes, same terms as
every team.

Sponsorship of catering or venue is welcome and acknowledged in the
recap. Let me know if any of those work.
```

Most reasonable vendors take this answer. The ones that don't were
not going to be good participants anyway.

## Internal Speakers (For Internal Chapters)

For an internal chapter, the keynote is sometimes given by:

- The executive sponsor.
- A senior auditor with something concrete to share.
- A peer chapter's captain, visiting.

Internal-speaker rules:

- The executive sponsor speaks at the *first* session of the year
  and the *last* session of the year. Not every session. Every-session
  presence makes them a fixture; once a year makes them
  important.
- A senior auditor speaks if they have something **specific** to
  share — a particular finding, a particular methodology decision.
  Not "my career in audit."
- A peer chapter's captain visiting is one of the most valuable
  speaker slots available — it is the moment the room sees the
  federation in person. Prioritise these when offered.

## After The Talk

The captain thanks the speaker briefly in the room (twenty seconds —
not a speech), introduces the next block, and moves on.

Within 24 hours, send the speaker a personal thank-you. Within seven
days, share the recap and any photos that include them. Speakers
remember being well-handled and tell other people.

A small ritual: when a speaker has been particularly valuable, the
captain notes them in the next quarterly readout to the sponsor /
endorsing body. Speaker quality is part of the chapter's track
record.
