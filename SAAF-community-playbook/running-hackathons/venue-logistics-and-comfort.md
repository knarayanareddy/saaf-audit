# Venue, Logistics, And Comfort

The unglamorous half of why sessions work.

If the day's content is excellent and the wifi is bad, you have a bad
session. If the agenda is rough and the room is comfortable, fed,
warm, and reachable, you have a session people remember fondly and
come back to.

Treat this page as a checklist. Go through it for every session, even
when you have done five before. Things change.

---

## Venue Selection

**Capacity and shape.** The room should fit the headcount with 30%
extra space. People build standing up, sitting on the floor, on
windowsills. A room "exactly the right size" is a room that feels
crowded by 11:00.

**Tables, not theatre.** Round or rectangular tables, 4–6 seats each.
Theatre seating breaks the format — there is nowhere to lay out
laptops, no shared screen orientation, no team boundary.

**Power.** This is the single most underestimated requirement. Plan
for **one outlet per person**, minimum. Most venues do not have
this. Bring or rent multi-socket extension strips — at least one
per table. Two is better.

> **Practical rule.** If a participant cannot keep their laptop
> charged through a 90-minute build sprint without standing up to
> hunt for an outlet, the venue is a problem. Outlets affect output
> more than any agenda decision.

**Wifi.** Wired connections are best for the AV / demo screen. Wifi
must:

- Reach every corner of the room.
- Handle 1.5x the headcount (people bring phones plus laptops).
- Allow outbound traffic to GitHub, npm, PyPI, Hugging Face, and
  major cloud APIs (some corporate venues block these).
- Have a single, simple password — not a captive portal that requires
  re-login every two hours.

If the venue's wifi is uncertain, bring a backup: a 4G/5G hotspot
that the operator carries, plus a list of the local mobile data
providers people can tether to.

**Daylight and air.** Rooms without windows kill afternoon energy.
Rooms with no air conditioning at full capacity are unbearable by
14:00. If you cannot have both daylight and good air, prioritise air
— a hot stuffy room with a view is worse than a cool windowless one.

**Acoustics.** Carpet, soft furnishings, and acoustic panels are
worth their weight. A glass-walled boardroom is a hostile environment
for ten teams talking at once.

**Bathrooms.** Plural. Within thirty seconds of the room. With the
right ratios for the headcount. This is genuinely a frequent
issue at venues built for smaller meetings.

**Quiet space.** A separate, quieter room or area where:
- People can take a phone call without disrupting the room.
- Neurodivergent participants can decompress.
- A team that needs to whiteboard quietly can step out.
- A nursing parent has somewhere private.

This room does not need to be large. It needs to exist, be
identified to the room in the welcome block, and be visibly calm.

**Directions and accessibility.** The venue is reachable by public
transport from at least one major hub in the region. The
"directions" block of your invitation includes:

- Nearest train/metro station and walking time.
- Nearest tram stop or bus stop, if relevant.
- Parking instructions, including cost and validation.
- Accessibility notes (lift, wheelchair access, accessible bathroom).
- The exact entrance to use (large buildings often have many).
- A phone number for "I'm lost" in the morning.

A clear map embedded in the invitation, not just a Google Maps link.
People print things; people lose phone signal.

## Capacity, RSVP, And The Waiting List

### Capacity is a hard number — agree it first

Before invitations go out, the captain agrees a **maximum headcount**
with the host venue and any sponsor underwriting catering. That
number is the cap. The chapter operates inside it; it does not
quietly grow past it.

The cap is set by whichever constraint binds first:

- Room capacity (seated, comfortable, not crammed).
- Outlet / wifi capacity (one outlet per person is the floor).
- Catering capacity (what the budget covers).
- Facilitator capacity (one facilitator per ~15 builders).

Write the cap into the operator's plan. Share it with the host and
sponsor so everyone knows the same number. **The cap is the working
constraint, not an aspiration to beat.**

### RSVP is via the calendar invitation

Members on the chapter's distribution list receive a **calendar
invitation** for each session. Accept / decline on that invitation is
the RSVP. There is no separate reconfirmation email — that's friction
nobody needs, and asking auditors to confirm twice trains them to
ignore the second ask.

| Calendar response | What it means | What the operator does |
|---|---|---|
| **Accept** | You are coming. Locked in. | Count toward headcount. Add to attendee list. |
| **Decline** | You are not coming. | Remove from headcount. Promote the next person from the waiting list if any. |
| **Tentative** | Ambiguous. | The operator sends a short personal email asking the person to clarify by a stated date. |
| **No response** | Treat as decline after the deadline. | Remove from headcount once the response deadline passes. |

The operator owns the RSVP tracker. The captain owns the cap.

### Tentative responses get a personal follow-up

Tentatives are the only category requiring outreach. The operator
emails each tentative respondent individually:

> *"Hi [Name] — you've marked tentative for [date]. Could you let me
> know by [deadline] whether you're coming? We have a capped
> headcount and a waiting list; if you can't make it, your spot
> goes to someone on the list."*

Two to three sentences, signed by the operator (not a no-reply
address). The "waiting list" framing is honest and effective — most
tentatives commit one way or the other when they realise the spot
has value to someone else.

### The waiting list

Once accepts plus expected tentative-yes responses reach the cap,
new sign-ups go on a **waiting list** rather than getting a calendar
invitation. Waiting-list mechanics:

- Position is by sign-up time, not seniority or organisation.
- The operator's tracker shows the waitlist as an ordered list.
- When someone declines (or a tentative says no), the next person
  on the waiting list gets a calendar invitation immediately —
  same channel, same wording, same accept-or-decline mechanic.
- If the waiting list is empty when a decline comes in, the
  session is simply slightly under capacity. That is fine.
- People stay on the waiting list across sessions if they want —
  ask them when they sign up.

The waiting list **is not** a way to oversubscribe a session. The
cap is the cap; the waiting list exists only to fill spots that
open up.

### Dietary needs and consent — collected once, on first sign-up

Dietary needs, photo consent, and accessibility needs are collected
**once**, when someone first signs up for the chapter's
distribution list (or on their first calendar invitation acceptance).
They are stored in the operator's contact list and pulled per
session — they do not need to be re-collected each time.

The structured dietary set to ask about:

- Vegetarian
- Vegan
- Halal
- Kosher
- Gluten-free
- Dairy-free
- Nut allergy
- Other (free text)

Dietary needs go to the caterer in the same format. Don't translate
between forms.

If someone's dietary needs change, they update the operator directly
— the operator maintains the source of truth, not a registration
form per session.

### What the operator does, week by week

- **Six weeks out.** Calendar invitation sent to the chapter
  distribution list. Cap stated in the invitation description.
  Sign-up link for non-members provided.
- **Four weeks out.** Operator checks current accepts vs cap. If
  the room is filling fast, the waiting-list mechanism is
  activated; later sign-ups are told they are on the waitlist.
- **One week out.** Tentatives get the personal follow-up email.
  Catering numbers locked based on accepts + (tentative-yes count
  × 0.5) + 10% buffer.
- **Two days out.** Waitlist promoted if any decline comes in
  during the final days. Final attendee list shared with the door
  / registration volunteer.
- **Within 3 days after.** No-shows logged in the attendance
  tracker. First-time no-shows get a friendly check-in. Repeat
  no-shows flagged for the captain (see below).

### No-shows and repeat no-shows

A no-show is not a moral failing. People get sick, miss trains,
have family emergencies, get pulled into work crises the night
before. The first time someone accepts and doesn't appear, the
right response is a kind, no-judgment check-in:

> *"Hi [Name] — we missed you at [date]. Hope everything's OK.
> Let me know if you'd like to be on the list for the next session,
> or if a different cadence would suit you better."*

That's it. One short note, signed by the operator. No
guilt-tripping. No request for explanation.

**Repeat no-shows are a different matter.** If someone has accepted
and not attended **three or more times in the last 12 months**, they
are taking spots from people on the waitlist who would have come.
The waiting-list mechanism — which protects access for everyone —
quietly stops working if repeat no-shows are tolerated.

After the third no-show within 12 months, the captain has a short,
private conversation with the person:

> *"Hi [Name] — I noticed you've accepted the last few invitations
> and weren't able to make it. No judgment from me, things happen.
> But we operate a capped headcount with a waiting list, and I want
> to be fair to people who are on the list.*
>
> *I'd like to move you to "waitlist by default" — you'd still get
> notified about each session and could opt in if a spot is open,
> but you wouldn't take an auto-accept spot from someone on the
> list. Does that work?*
>
> *If the chapter's cadence isn't working for you any more, that's
> also a fine answer — and we'd love to keep you on the recap
> distribution either way."*

The conversation is private (one-on-one, not in the channel) and
ends with the person's consent to one of three outcomes:

1. **Waitlist by default.** The person stays on the chapter
   distribution but their default RSVP becomes "waitlist." They
   can still opt in per session if they have certainty.
2. **Recap-only.** The person is removed from the calendar
   invitation list but stays on the recap distribution. They can
   ask back in any time.
3. **Off the chapter list entirely.** Rare, and only at the
   person's request.

Tracking and rhythm:

- The **attendance tracker** is a simple spreadsheet: one row per
  member, columns for each session showing accepted / attended /
  declined / no-show / waitlist.
- The **12-month window** rolls — a no-show in session N falls off
  the count once four quarterly sessions have passed.
- The **three-strike threshold** is the recommended default, not an
  absolute. Use judgment: someone whose three no-shows were all
  during a known life event (parental leave, a serious illness)
  doesn't need the conversation.
- The conversation is **not a punishment** — it is the captain
  protecting the integrity of the waiting list. Frame it that way.

The single best thing the chapter can do to prevent no-shows in
the first place: keep the waiting list visible. When members know
their accepted spot has value to someone else, no-show rates drop
without any policy enforcement at all.

**Label every dish on the day.** Allergens visible on each tray, not
in a binder by the door. Vegan and vegetarian dishes labelled. The
person with the nut allergy should not have to ask anyone what is in
the salad.

## Coffee And Tea — The Whole Day

A SAAF day is a long day on people's brains. Coffee, tea, water, and
something sweet are continuously available from 08:30 to 17:00.

| Time | What's available |
|---|---|
| 08:30 | Hot coffee, tea, water, light pastry / fruit / yoghurt |
| 09:45 | Coffee break — fresh refill, water, light snacks |
| 11:00 | Second coffee break — fresh refill, fruit, biscuits |
| 12:00 | Lunch (see below) |
| 14:30 | Coffee break during the hackathon — fresh refill |
| 15:30 | Discreet snack table appears: chocolate, fruit, nuts (allergy-permitting), small sandwiches |
| 16:00 | Coffee station refreshed for the demo session |
| 17:00 | Drinks. Sharp start. |

**Decaf, herbal tea, and good water are not optional.** A non-trivial
share of any audit population doesn't drink caffeine after lunch and
suffers if their only options are espresso or fizzy drinks.

**Reusable cups or proper crockery.** Disposable cups are
unpleasant at the seventh refill and make the room look like a fast-
food restaurant by 15:00. Most venues will provide ceramic cups if
asked; if not, bring them.

## Lunch

Lunch is its own test of the day. Get it wrong and the afternoon is
sluggish; get it right and people thank you in the recap survey.

**Light over heavy.** Salad bowls, grain bowls, sandwiches, soup,
fruit. Not three-course hot meals. Heavy lunch + the afternoon
hackathon = nobody finishes anything. The afternoon build window is
already tight; teams need clear heads at 13:00.

**Variety covering all dietary needs.** Two of every dietary
category (vegetarian, vegan, halal, gluten-free) so someone with a
restriction is not stuck with one option.

**Served all at once if possible.** Long lunch queues kill the social
function of the lunch break. Buffet over plated. Multiple stations
over a single line.

**No formal seating plan.** Lunch is for cross-pollination; let
people pick their own tables, but verbally encourage mixing in the
12:55 announcement.

**Allergens labelled.** Already said this. Saying it again because
it's the most common venue failure.

## Snacks

Snack table appears at 15:30, discreetly, mid-way through build
sprint 2. They smell it; they wander over; they get sugar; they
return to their teams. The right shape:

- Small sandwiches or bao buns, cut in half (one snack-sized portion
  per piece).
- Fresh fruit — apples, bananas, grapes, berries.
- Chocolate, biscuits, energy bars.
- Nuts, *labelled visibly*, kept separate from the rest of the table
  for nut-allergy participants.
- Water and refreshed hot drinks.

Snacks should not require a dedicated person watching them. They are
a self-service energy refill. Refresh once at ~16:00 if they are
running low.

## Drinks At The End

Drinks begin **at 17:00 sharp** — the moment the closing block ends.
Their job is to let conversations that started at lunch finish, and
to let new conversations start that weren't possible during the
afternoon build time.

The right shape:

- Beer, wine, soft drinks, water — sized for ~80% of headcount times
  two drinks per person.
- A non-alcoholic option that is not just sad orange juice. Quality
  alcohol-free beer or a proper soft drink.
- Light food: olives, bread, cheese, charcuterie, crudités. Not a
  second meal — a graze table.
- A clearly stated end time, typically 19:00 or 19:30. Drinks that
  drift past 20:00 burn out the operators.

The captain and founding team are circulating, not clustering. The
operator is winding down the venue, not socialising — they are the
person making sure the venue is empty by the agreed end time.

## Speakers In The Morning

Reach out **early** for morning speakers — internally and externally.
Lead time: six weeks for an external keynote, four weeks for an
internal one. Speakers worth having are busy; speakers who are
available on three days' notice are usually a sign.

The brief to a speaker is short and explicit:

- Who the audience is (auditors, Professional Practices, analysts, engineers).
- The session theme.
- The hard time limit (twenty minutes for a keynote, ten for an
  introduction).
- What the slot is for ("frame the day, not promote a product").
- The speaker's specific value-add (why we asked them, not someone
  else).

See [speakers-and-keynotes.md](speakers-and-keynotes.md) for more.

## Volunteers And Operations Team

A 50-person session needs at least three operators on the day:

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Operator-in-charge** | Owns logistics. Signs for catering, briefs venue staff, watches the wifi, manages timing. |
| **Door / registration** | Welcomes participants, hands out badges, points to the room, answers "where's the bathroom" until 09:30. |
| **Floater** | Spare hands. Helps the demo session (laptops, cables), runs the snack refresh, takes photos with consent. |

Volunteers, not the captain. The captain is in the room. If the
captain is also the operator, the captain is too distracted to
defend the manifesto — and that is when sessions drift.

For 80+ people: add a second floater and consider a dedicated AV
person for the demo session.

## Photo And Recording Policy

Decide before the day:

- Are photos allowed? (Default: yes, but ask at registration; some
  participants prefer not to be photographed for legitimate reasons.)
- Are demos recorded? (Default: yes for highlights, with consent of
  the demoing team.)
- Will any of this be public? (Default: aggregated public; specific
  individuals only with their explicit consent.)

The policy goes in the registration form so people can opt out.
There is a clear "do not photograph" sticker option at the
registration desk for people who opted out, or for people who change
their minds on the day.

## What To Pack

The operator's bag, every session:

- Multiple multi-socket extension strips.
- A roll of gaffer tape (cables to floors, signs to walls).
- HDMI, USB-C, and DisplayPort adapters for the demo screen.
- A wired mouse and keyboard, for the rare laptop emergency.
- Laser pointer / clicker.
- A first-aid kit.
- A box of pens and Post-its.
- Printed copies of: agenda, manifesto (for the back of the table
  signs), wifi password, dietary-need labels, "do not photograph"
  stickers.
- A spare phone charger.
- The venue contact phone numbers.

A 50-person session can collapse if the demo screen has the wrong
adapter and the operator has to drive to a shop. Bring the bag.

## After The Day

Within an hour of doors closing:

- All recoverable laptops, chargers, and stationery are reunited
  with their owners.
- The room is left in the state the venue requested.
- Photos and demo recordings are uploaded to wherever they live.

Within 24 hours:

- Thank-you note to the venue contact, the catering contact, and the
  AV / IT contact.
- Quick survey to participants (5 questions max) — this becomes
  input for [post-event-recap.md](post-event-recap.md).

Within 7 days:

- The recap is written and shared. See
  [post-event-recap.md](post-event-recap.md).

## A Final Test

Before each session, do a walk-through with the operator the day
before, in the actual room. Plug in a laptop. Walk to the bathroom.
Find the quiet space. Check that the wifi works in the corner of
the room furthest from the router. Set up a multi-socket extension
on every table.

The walk-through finds the things the booking PDF doesn't. Every
operator has a story about the venue that didn't have the outlets it
promised, or the wifi that worked only in half the room. The walk-
through prevents those stories.
