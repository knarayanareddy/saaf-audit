# Facilitation

How to run the day in the room. The skill of getting forty to a
hundred people into productive teams, keeping them there, rescuing
the stuck, slowing down the over-confident, and ending on time.

This page is for the captain and for track leads / facilitators. Read
it before your first time running a session, and again before each
session for the first year.

---

## What Facilitation Is

Facilitation at a SAAF session is mostly *invisible*. When facilitation
is working, the captain looks like they're standing around chatting.
When facilitation is failing, the captain is on stage giving
announcements.

The good captain:

- Walks the room continuously during build sprints.
- Knows every team's plan and every team's stuck point by lunch.
- Has the sentence "what are you working on?" memorised in three
  variants depending on the team's mood.
- Is willing to interrupt themselves to handle a small thing now.
- Is willing to leave a stuck team alone for an extra fifteen minutes
  if the team is *productively* stuck.

---

## The Roles On The Day

| Role | Number | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Captain** | 1 | Holds the day. Frames each block. Defends time limits. |
| **Co-captain** | 1 | Mirrors the captain. Steps in for blocks the captain can't run. |
| **Track lead** | 1 per track | Walks their track. Knows every team's plan. Resolves stuck moments. |
| **Operator-in-charge** | 1 | Owns logistics (see [venue-logistics-and-comfort.md](venue-logistics-and-comfort.md)). |
| **Recap writer** | 1 | Documents the day. Cannot also build. |
| **Floater** | 1+ | Spare hands for the operator. |

For a 50-person session: 1 captain, 1 co-captain, 3 track leads, 1
operator, 1 recap writer, 1 floater. Seven people minimum on the
operating side.

## The Day's Cadence For The Captain

The captain's day, hour by hour:

```
07:30  Arrive. Walk the room with the operator. Confirm AV, wifi, outlets.
08:00  Brief the volunteers. Walk through the day's agenda once.
08:30  Stand near the door. Greet arriving participants by name where possible.
09:00  Welcome.
09:15  Hand off to keynote speaker. Run the timer.
09:45  Coffee break. Be visible near the table.
10:00  Hand off to Presentation 1. Run the timer.
10:30  Hand off to Presentation 2. Run the timer.
11:00  Coffee break. Confirm plans / tracks one last time with track leads.
11:15  Plan walkthrough. Lead the claim moment toward lunch.
12:00  Lunch. Move between tables; help teams that haven't yet formed.
13:00  Hackathon kicks off. Walk every team in the first 30 min of sprint 1.
14:30  Coffee break. Visit the teams who looked stuck. Get them unstuck.
14:45  Sprint 2. Heaviest walking block. Energy management.
15:30  Snack table refresh. Quick word with the recap writer about what's important.
15:45  Announce "fifteen minutes until demos." Help teams compress.
16:00  Demo session. Captain runs the timer.
16:45  Closing.
17:00  Drinks. Captain circulates. Sharp end at 17:00 — drinks begin.
19:00  Drinks end. Final check with operator.
```

The pattern: *frame the block, then walk the room*. The captain is
not on stage during build sprints. They are in the room, present and
quiet.

## Walking The Room

Walking is the captain's most important skill. The questions to ask
(rotate; do not interrogate):

| Question | When to ask it | What you're listening for |
|---|---|---|
| *"What are you building?"* | First sprint, every team | Whether the team can articulate their use case |
| *"What's blocking you?"* | Every sprint | Practical blockers — wifi, missing teammate, missing API key |
| *"Who's not at this table that you wish was?"* | Sprint 2 (early) | Cross-track collaboration opportunities |
| *"Walk me through what you've got so far."* | Sprint 2 (late) | A demo rehearsal, kinder than asking "is your demo ready?" |
| *"What are you planning to show in the demo?"* | 30 min before demos | Captures whether the team has scoped to a demo-able artefact |

The captain stays for two to four minutes per team in a 90-minute
sprint. Twenty teams × three minutes = 60 minutes of walking, leaving
30 minutes for individual conversations and crisis management.

## Reading The Room

Energy management is the next most important skill. Signals:

| Signal | Meaning | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple teams have shifted to laptops-only, conversation has dropped | Productive deep work | Don't interrupt |
| Multiple teams are on phones, not laptops | Stuck or bored | Walk over, ask questions |
| Same team is silent for an hour | Could be either; check | Walk over, gentle prompt |
| Loud team dominating one corner | Possibly bullying; possibly fun | Listen from a distance, intervene if dynamic is one-way |
| Visible frustration in a participant's face | Real problem | Pull them aside privately, ask if they want to talk |

The captain is not a therapist. The captain is a host. Their job is
to notice and to act, not to fix everything personally. Track leads
exist for that reason.

## Defending The Time Limits

The agenda only works if the time limits hold. The captain's
authority on time is the only thing standing between a 30-minute
keynote and a 45-minute keynote.

Practical patterns:

- **Visible countdown timer** for keynotes and demos. Everyone sees
  it. The speaker sees it.
- **Soft signal at five minutes remaining** — captain stands at the
  back of the room, hand raised. The speaker sees and adjusts.
- **Hard signal at zero** — captain walks to the front, says
  "thanks, [name], let's give them a hand," and moves to the next
  block.
- **Cut a speaker only with respect.** Not "you're out of time" —
  "thank you, that was a great twenty minutes, let's hear from the
  next team."

Speakers who go over once will go over again. Don't soften the
signal because you're worried about offending. Soften the *delivery*,
not the principle.

## Rescuing A Stuck Team

By 14:30 (end of build sprint 1 in the afternoon hackathon), at least
one team will be stuck. The diagnosis hierarchy:

1. **Are they stuck on scope?** They've been arguing about what to
   build for an hour. Move: a track lead sits with them for ten
   minutes, helps them carve a 90-minute MVP from the larger plan.
   Then leaves.

2. **Are they stuck on tech?** A specific API doesn't work, a model
   isn't responding, a credential is missing. Move: pair them with
   the engineer from another team for fifteen minutes. People love
   helping with concrete tech problems; they hate helping with
   scope.

3. **Are they stuck on each other?** A dynamic isn't working. One
   person is dominating, or two people disagree about direction.
   Move: the track lead pulls one of them aside privately at the
   14:30 break, asks how it's going, listens. Often a quiet word
   resolves what the team can't.

4. **Are they stuck because the plan is wrong?** The plan looked
   good on paper but doesn't survive contact with the data. Move:
   re-scope to a smaller artefact (a single skill, not a full agent),
   document what was learned about the plan being wrong, demo the
   smaller artefact at 16:00. The recap writer notes the plan needs
   re-scoping.

If a team is still stuck after the 14:30 break, dissolve and re-form.
Frame it gently: *"We're going to try a different combination for
this last sprint — [Person] and [Person] are joining you to work on
[adjacent plan]."* Two of the team's members carry forward to a
different plan; the others fold into another team. This is rare but
legitimate. It's better than three people staring at a blank screen
until 16:00.

## Energy In The Room

The afternoon dip is real. Around 15:00, energy drops. Counter-
moves the captain has at their disposal:

- **Sugar.** The 15:30 snack table refresh is partly an energy
  intervention.
- **Stand-up moments.** "Stand up, walk to a team that isn't yours,
  spend five minutes hearing what they're building." Forces movement
  and cross-pollination.
- **Music.** Quiet, instrumental, in the background. Adjusts the
  room's energy without disrupting concentration. Can be on a
  playlist the operator manages.
- **A visible countdown.** "Ninety minutes until demos. Eighty
  minutes. Seventy minutes." Helps focus.
- **Direct conversation.** If a team is visibly dragging, the captain
  asks: *"What would help right now?"* The answers vary; offering
  the question matters.

## When Something Goes Wrong

Things will go wrong. The wifi will fail. A keynote speaker will
cancel two days before. A vendor will become aggressive. A
participant will have a personal crisis.

The captain's principles:

- **Name the problem to yourself first.** Don't react before
  diagnosing.
- **Triage in order: people, then content, then logistics.** A
  participant in distress comes before a broken demo screen.
- **Delegate to the right role.** The operator handles wifi. The
  co-captain handles a stuck team. The captain handles a dispute.
  The captain does not do all three at once.
- **Communicate to the room.** If something visible breaks, the
  captain acknowledges it and says what's being done. Nothing
  unsettles a room faster than an unspoken problem.
- **After the day, write down what happened.** A short post-mortem
  for yourself. Patterns emerge across sessions.

## Training A New Facilitator

A track lead becoming a facilitator should:

- Shadow the captain for one full session before facilitating.
- Co-facilitate with the captain or co-captain for the next session.
- Solo-facilitate a track for a session, with the captain available
  if needed.
- Become a candidate for co-captain after that.

The pipeline matters. A chapter that promotes too quickly produces
under-prepared facilitators; a chapter that doesn't promote
produces a captain bottleneck. The middle path — three sessions of
deliberate ramp — is the right one.

---

## A Final Note

Facilitation is the part of the chapter that you cannot read your way
into being good at. You learn it by doing it, badly the first time,
and reflecting on what didn't work.

After every session, the captain should write down — privately, in
ten lines — what worked, what didn't, and what to try next time. Re-
read those notes before the next session. The chapter improves
through this loop more than through any of the formal mechanisms
above.
