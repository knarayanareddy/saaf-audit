# Recognition And Rituals

How to acknowledge contribution without turning the chapter into a
points-and-prizes game.

The community-engagement literature consistently flags one finding:
contributions that are **visible and connected to outcomes** keep
coming. Contributions that are invisible stop. The captain's job is
to make contribution visible — and to do so in a way that respects
the audit profession's adult, professional culture.

---

## Three Currencies

Members are paid in three currencies for the work they put into the
chapter. Pick the right one for the moment; don't substitute.

### Visibility

The contributor's name on the artefact, in the recap, in the room.

- Their name on the plan they wrote.
- Their name in the recap as the demoer.
- Their name on the leaderboard (with caveats — see below).
- Their introduction to the room as track lead.

Visibility is the most-used currency and the cheapest to provide.
Done well, it is also the most durable.

### Learning

First access to new tooling, mentorship from senior community
members, invitations to advanced sessions or workshops.

- A regional chapter member invited to attend a main SAAF
  Project session as an observer.
- A new contributor paired with a senior contributor on their first
  plan.
- Early-access invitations to a beta of a tool the chapter is
  evaluating.

Learning is the most desired currency by ambitious contributors —
they are at SAAF partly to grow.

### Access

To the captain, to sponsors, to the endorsing body, to the national
SAAF Project organisers.

- A direct line to the captain when something needs to escalate.
- An introduction to a sponsor's senior contact.
- A speaking slot at the IIA / ISACA / NOREA equivalent event.

Access is the rarest currency and should be used deliberately. Don't
over-issue access; it loses meaning.

## What Recognition Looks Like

### After a contribution

Within seven days of a session, every named contributor receives a
named acknowledgement in the recap. Not a generic "thanks to all
participants" line. Specific, with the contribution called out.

> *"The vendor risk pre-screen agent was built by [Names]. It
> processed the synthetic test set in 4 minutes vs. our previous
> baseline of 4 hours per supplier. The team is running a Check
> stage at [Organisation] in Q3."*

Three sentences. Specific names, specific contribution, what's next.
This is the minimum bar.

### After a sustained period of contribution

When a champion has been carrying weight for a quarter or more, the
acknowledgement gets bigger:

- A specific paragraph in the quarterly readout to the sponsor /
  endorsing body, naming them.
- A direct thank-you from the captain, in writing, that the
  champion can quote in their own performance review.
- An invitation to the next role tier (or, if they're already there,
  an invitation to mentor a newer champion).

### When a champion steps back

A graceful exit is a form of recognition. Done well, it makes the
champion want to come back.

- The captain thanks them publicly in the next recap, framing it as
  *completion*, not *exit*.
- Their name stays on every artefact they built. Forever.
- They are kept in the channel, the mailing list, and the
  invitations — unless they ask to be removed.
- They are invited back warmly when they signal interest.

## The Leaderboard Question

The main SAAF Project maintains a **federation-wide leaderboard**
through the [SAAF Portal](../measuring-value/saaf-portal.md). It is
useful — and it is also a tool that needs handling.

**Useful, because:**

- It makes contribution visible in a way the recap alone cannot.
- It surfaces under-credited contributors that the captain might
  miss in narrative form.
- It signals to managers what their teams are contributing.
- It is **automatically populated** from portal activity (plan PRs,
  observability checks, badges), so it is consistent across chapters
  and removes any captain-favouritism concern.

**Things to watch:**

- A leaderboard turns contribution into a competition. Audit
  professionals are competitive; the chapter is supposed to be the
  place where they aren't.
- Leaderboards reward *legible* contribution and undervalue
  *invisible* contribution (review, mentorship, conflict resolution).
- Leaderboards create gaming behaviour. People submit thin plans
  to bump their count.

How a captain handles the portal leaderboard well:

- **Use it to spot under-credited contributors**, then thank them
  by name in the recap. The leaderboard is the source of leads, not
  the recognition itself.
- **Quote it sparingly in chapter communications.** A rolling
  "thanks to our top contributors this quarter" line is fine; a
  weekly ranked rundown is not.
- **Never quote a single individual at #1 in public** in a way that
  positions them above peers. Show the top cluster, not a hero.
- **Do not build a parallel chapter-internal leaderboard.** The
  federation has one. A chapter-internal duplicate fragments
  recognition and undermines federation alignment.
- **Pair leaderboard mentions with named acknowledgement of
  invisible work** — the reviewers, mentors, and conflict-resolvers
  whose contributions the badge / leaderboard system can't see.

When the leaderboard is not enough, **the named credits in the
recap and the personal thank-you note** are still the captain's
strongest tools. The portal leaderboard supplements those; it
doesn't replace them.

## Rituals

Small, recurring rituals do more for chapter cohesion than most
explicit recognition programmes. The ones that have worked at SAAF NL
sessions and are worth replicating:

### The closing thanks

At the end of every session, the captain names the host organisation,
the speakers, the volunteers, and *every team*. Not a list of
participants — a list of teams, with their plan and their progress.
Five minutes. The room hears its own work spoken back to it.

### The first-timer welcome

At every session, the captain spends thirty seconds asking first-
timers to raise their hands and welcoming them. Not a long speech.
Acknowledgement. The first-timers are visible to the rest of the
room and feel less like they're crashing a private party.

### The seven-day note

Within seven days of every session, the captain sends a personal
note to two or three contributors who did something specific that
mattered. Not a mass email — a personal one. Two paragraphs. *"What
you did at the session this weekend was [X]. It mattered because
[Y]. Thank you, and I'd love to invite you to [Z]."*

This is the highest-ROI thing a captain does. It costs an hour of
the captain's time and builds champions for the next year.

### The plan-completion announcement

When a plan reaches Act, the captain announces it in the channel and
in the next recap. Specifically. *"Plan X is complete. Built by
[Names], piloted at [Organisation], lessons learned filed at [URL].
Thanks to all involved."*

Plan completion is the chapter's most important success signal. Make
the announcement of completion feel like a ritual, not an
afterthought.

### The annual roll call

Once a year, the chapter publishes a roll-call list — every member
who contributed to the chapter in any tier in the past year. By name
(with consent and pseudonyms preserved). With a one-line summary of
their contribution.

This is read more than any other artefact the chapter produces. It is
the document people forward to their managers. It is the document
new candidates read before deciding whether to attend.

## Anti-Patterns

### The certificates-and-pins gimmick

Some chapters issue certificates, pins, or badges to "champions."
This works for some communities. It tends not to work for audit
communities — the profession already runs on certifications, and
chapter-issued ones feel diluting.

If you must issue tangible recognition, make it small, specific, and
non-transferable. A printed letter from the captain. A name on a
permanent contributor wall. Something that has meaning *only* in
this community.

### The thanks-everyone email

Generic emails ("thanks to everyone who came!") don't recognise
anyone. They are a captain-comfort artefact, not a recognition
artefact. Skip them. Send three personal notes instead.

### The over-thanking spiral

If every contribution gets a public thank-you, public thank-yous
stop meaning anything. Calibrate. A small contribution gets a small
acknowledgement (a named line in the recap). A large contribution
gets a large one (a paragraph, a personal note, a speaking slot).

### The recognition delegated to the executive

Internal chapters sometimes funnel all recognition through the
executive sponsor. This sounds like a feature; it isn't. Recognition
that comes only from above feels political. The most valuable
recognition is from peers. The captain's note matters more than the
sponsor's.

## A Recognition Audit

Once a quarter, the captain reviews the past three months and asks:

1. *Who contributed substantively that I haven't acknowledged?*
2. *Whose contribution have I over-acknowledged, in a way that
   might feel performative?*
3. *Who needs a step up — into a role, into visibility, into access
   — that I haven't offered?*
4. *Who is doing invisible work — review, mentorship, conflict
   resolution — that the public recognition misses?*

Answer these honestly. The fourth is the hardest and most
important. Invisible contribution is the easiest to lose; once
people stop doing it, the chapter notices only when something quietly
fails.
