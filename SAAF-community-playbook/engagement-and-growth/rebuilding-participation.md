# Rebuilding Participation

Participation will drop. This page is about recognising it early,
reaching out without pretending the drop didn't happen, and rebuilding
without forcing it.

---

## Why Participation Drops

Participation declines for predictable reasons. Knowing which one
you're facing changes the response.

| Cause | Signal |
|---|---|
| **Captain or champion turnover** | A specific person left and the conversations that depended on them stopped. |
| **Cadence too high** | People are still interested but exhausted. Each session feels like a tax. |
| **Cadence too low** | People forget the chapter exists between sessions; momentum dies. |
| **Format drift** | Sessions stopped feeling like the SAAF format; people lose the original reason they came. |
| **Plan graveyard** | Plans accumulated without completing; builders feel their work doesn't land. |
| **Personal life events** | A specific contributor's life changed — a job move, parental leave, a health issue. |
| **Vendor capture** | Sessions feel like vendor showcases; cross-vendor participants leave. |
| **Internal political shift** | At an internal chapter, the executive sponsor moved on or lost air cover. |

Most participation drops have multiple causes layered. The discipline
is to diagnose, not to assume.

---

## Recognising The Decline Early

Borrowing from the engagement playbook: leading indicators move
before lagging indicators. Watch for:

- **Channel quietness.** The chapter's communication channel goes
  from a few messages a week to a few messages a month.
- **Pre-session RSVP drop.** Registration tracks well below previous
  sessions at the same point in the cycle.
- **Plan submissions declining.** Fewer plans being submitted in the
  weeks before a session.
- **Same-faces phenomenon.** The same five contributors are doing
  everything; new faces don't appear.
- **Demos that aren't demos.** At sessions, teams show prototypes
  more than working artefacts. Build time is being eaten by other
  things.

These are signals to act on now, not at the next quarterly review.

---

## The First Move: Listen

Before launching a re-engagement campaign, talk to people. Not in
the channel — privately, one-on-one.

Reach out to five to seven people from across the funnel:

- A contributor who has gone quiet.
- A champion who is still showing up but seems tired.
- A participant who used to attend regularly and stopped.
- A first-timer from the last session who didn't come back.
- A founding partner whose attendance has tapered.

Ask one question, in different forms:

> *"How are you finding the chapter at the moment? What's working,
> what isn't?"*

Listen. Take notes. Resist the temptation to defend or explain.
Pattern-match across the conversations.

A useful framing: *analytics tell you engagement is dropping, but
members tell you why.* The analytics are the symptom; the
conversations are the diagnosis.

---

## The Second Move: Match Response To Cause

The diagnosis from listening determines what you do.

### If the cause is captain or champion turnover

Do not try to "rebuild engagement" generically. The chapter has lost
a person, and the conversations that depended on them stopped. The
response is **succession**, not engagement campaigns.

- Name the new captain or champion publicly and quickly.
- Invite the previous one back as alumnus, with public thanks.
- Identify the specific conversations that died with them and pick
  up at least one or two.

### If the cause is cadence

If too high: cut to the lower cadence (monthly → quarterly,
quarterly → biannual). Communicate the change and the reason. Do not
apologise — frame it as a refocus.

If too low: increase the *between-session* touchpoints (a monthly
demo, a fortnightly digest), not the major-session frequency.

### If the cause is format drift

Re-read [running-hackathons/format-and-agenda.md](../running-hackathons/format-and-agenda.md)
and [foundations/what-saaf-is.md](../foundations/what-saaf-is.md)
together. Identify how sessions have drifted.

Common drifts: too many keynotes, not enough build time; too many
participants without enough builders; too much scope per plan.

The fix is structural — change the format of the next session,
restore the build-time floor, communicate the change as a return to
fundamentals.

### If the cause is the plan graveyard

This is one of the most demoralising drops. Builders see their work
not landing and conclude the chapter isn't a serious vehicle.

The response: pick three plans that are stuck in Do/Check, allocate
real resources to push them to Act, and announce the pushes
publicly. The chapter rebuilds trust by closing loops, not by
launching new initiatives.

Read [internal-chapter/pitfalls.md](../internal-chapter/pitfalls.md)
for the specific pattern.

### If the cause is personal life events

You cannot fix this, and you should not try. The right move is *low
pressure outreach*, not engagement campaigns.

A short, personal note. Something like:

> *"I noticed you haven't been around the last two sessions. No
> pressure at all — I just wanted to check in. We'd love to have
> you back when life lets you, and the chapter's here whenever
> that is."*

Some people come back in two months. Some come back in two years.
Some never come back. All three are fine. Forced re-engagement
campaigns turn the soft "not now" into a hard "no."

### If the cause is vendor capture

See [internal-chapter/pitfalls.md](../internal-chapter/pitfalls.md#5-the-slow-motion-vendor-capture)
and [regional-chapter/identity-and-positioning.md](../regional-chapter/identity-and-positioning.md).
The fix is structural — re-balance sponsorship, rotate venues away
from a captured host, and make the next session visibly vendor-
neutral.

### If the cause is internal political shift

For an internal chapter: read
[internal-chapter/building-internal-alignment.md](../internal-chapter/building-internal-alignment.md).
The chapter cannot survive without sponsor air cover; getting a new
sponsor on board is an existential priority.

## The Third Move: Empathetic Outreach

For the people you want back, the outreach is **personal,
unautomated, specific**. Not a "we miss you!" newsletter.

The opening line that consistently works: *"I saw you haven't been
around lately and wanted to check in."* No more, no less.

Adapt to SAAF:

> *"Hi [Name] — saw your name on the [plan] from session 3 and
> wanted to ping you. We're working on [related plan] at the next
> session and I thought of your work. No pressure if it's not the
> right time, but you'd be welcome and your earlier work would land
> well. Let me know either way?"*

Three things this does:

1. References specifically what they contributed before.
2. Connects them to a *specific* upcoming reason to engage.
3. Makes "no" easy and unembarrassing.

Send these one at a time, not as a batch. They take longer; they
work better.

---

## The Fourth Move: Micro-Contribution Re-Entry

People returning after a gap usually don't want to start with
"facilitate a track at the next session." Offer them small re-entry
points:

- *"Could you review one plan for the next session? Twenty minutes.
  I'd value your perspective on this one specifically because [X]."*
- *"We'd love a one-paragraph note from you on the [topic] for the
  recap — your earlier work on it is the canonical reference."*
- *"Want to come to the drinks after the next session, even if you
  don't make the day? It'd be good to see you."*

Bounded asks. Specific, named, scoped. Micro-actions create faster,
more sustainable re-engagement than any big return moment.

---

## When Recognition Of A Returning Member Matters

When a previously-active member returns after an absence, recognise
it — but don't make a fuss. The right note in the recap:

> *"Welcome back to [Name], who reviewed plans for this session
> after a quiet quarter."*

One sentence. Names the contribution, not the absence. Returning
members feel seen without feeling examined.

The wrong note:

> *"After missing the last two sessions, [Name] is back!"*

Even with friendly intent, this draws attention to the absence and
makes future members nervous about taking time off.

---

## When To Accept The Decline

Sometimes a chapter is past the point of recovery. Signals:

- Captain has tried two distinct re-engagement approaches without
  effect.
- The active core is fewer than three people.
- The endorsing body has not renewed for the year.
- The most recent session had fewer than fifteen attendees and no
  working demos.
- Sponsor or budget has been withdrawn.

If three or more of these are true, the chapter may be at end of
life. The right move is to **pause or wind down explicitly**, not to
keep limping forward.

A graceful pause:

- Captain announces a pause to the chapter, with a clear *why*.
- Existing artefacts are archived in a stable location.
- Contributors are thanked individually.
- A path is left open for revival — "if anyone wants to take this
  on, here's what's there."

A pause is not a failure. Communities have life cycles. Acknowledging
the cycle preserves the dignity of everyone who contributed.

---

## A Health Check, Quarterly

Once a quarter, the captain reviews:

1. *Compared to the same quarter last year, are we contracting,
   stable, or growing? On which dimension — attendance, plans,
   completed plans?*
2. *Which signals from the "early decline" list are present?*
3. *Have I had a one-on-one in the last 30 days with any
   contributor whose engagement seems to have dropped?*
4. *What is the next concrete move I would make if engagement drops
   another 15% next quarter?*

Answer the fourth question even if engagement is fine. Having a
mental rehearsal of the response before you need it makes the
response faster and calmer.
