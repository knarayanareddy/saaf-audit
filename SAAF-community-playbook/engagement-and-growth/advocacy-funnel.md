# The Advocacy Funnel

How members move from "attended once" to "champion." The shape is the
standard community advocacy funnel, applied to the SAAF context.

The funnel exists not as a metric to optimise, but as a vocabulary
that lets the captain see who is in the chapter, where they are, and
what would help them go a step deeper if they want to.

The most important corrective to "funnel thinking" is this: **most
members are exactly where they want to be.** The chapter's job is not
to push every member to the top of the funnel. It is to ensure the
*path* is clear for those who want it.

---

## The Four Stages

```
Participant   →   Contributor   →   Ambassador   →   Champion
(attends,         (writes a         (represents       (helps run
listens,          plan, reviews,    chapter           the chapter,
asks)             demos)            externally)       owns work)
```

| Stage | Behaviour | Signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Participant** | Attends sessions. Reads recaps. Asks questions in the channel. | Attendance at 2+ sessions. Engagement with comms. |
| **Contributor** | Writes plans. Reviews other plans. Demos at sessions. Pairs on builds. | Named on a plan. Demoed at a session. Reviewed plans. |
| **Ambassador** | Speaks externally about the chapter. Recruits new members. Writes about the work. | Brought new participants. Spoke about chapter at a body's event. Wrote a blog or post. |
| **Champion** | Holds part of the chapter — track lead, facilitator, recap writer, captain. | Owns recurring work. Trusted to defend the manifesto live. |

The stages are not linear obligations. A participant can be a participant
forever. A contributor can step back to participant when life requires
it. The funnel describes options; it does not impose a ladder.

---

## What Each Stage Looks Like

### Participant

Most people in the chapter are participants. They attend sessions when
they can, read recaps, and benefit from the chapter without feeling a
duty to give back. This is healthy and expected.

What participants need from the chapter:

- A clear "what is the chapter and what's at the next session"
  message a few weeks in advance.
- An easy registration with no soft pressure.
- A recap they can read without attending.
- Permission to lurk. Not every member has to contribute.

What goes wrong: chapters that pressure participants to "get more
involved" lose them. Pressure breaks the implicit contract — that the
chapter is opt-in, not subscription-with-renewal.

### Contributor

Contributors have crossed an invisible threshold: they have produced
something that has their name on it. A plan, a prompt, a demo, a
review.

What contributors need:

- Recognition for their contribution, public and proportionate.
- A clear path to the next contribution if they want it.
- Feedback on what they contributed, especially when something didn't
  work.
- Direct exposure to other contributors — not always just through
  the captain.

What goes wrong: contributors whose work is invisible drift back to
participant. Every contribution should have a named acknowledgement
within seven days.

### Ambassador

Ambassadors represent the chapter outside its own walls. They bring
colleagues. They speak about the work at IIA / ISACA / NOREA events.
They write a LinkedIn post about a session that someone in another
country reads and joins.

What ambassadors need:

- Materials to point at — a one-page chapter description, a recap,
  a manifesto.
- Permission to speak — explicit, in writing, with the captain's
  thanks.
- A direct line to the captain, so they can flag inbound interest.
- The chapter's gratitude, expressed in the recap.

What goes wrong: ambassadors who feel like unpaid PR. The chapter
must contribute back to them — visibility, learning, access. See
[recognition-and-rituals.md](recognition-and-rituals.md).

### Champion

Champions hold parts of the chapter. They are the people the captain
calls when something needs doing.

The role tier (track lead, facilitator, recap writer, co-captain,
captain) is the *what*. Being a champion is the *who* — the cohort of
people the chapter relies on.

What champions need, in three words: **trust, investment, recognition.**
In SAAF terms:

- Real work to do — not symbolic roles.
- Budget for their development — training, travel, paid time.
- Their name on the artefacts they build.
- A graceful exit when their life or career demands it. See
  [rebuilding-participation.md](rebuilding-participation.md).
- A successor named before they need one.

What goes wrong: champions burned out by being asked for everything.
The captain's job is to prevent this — to spread the work, to
pre-empt overload, to release champions when they need release.

## Moving Between Stages

People move between stages through *direct exposure*, not promotion or
pressure. In SAAF terms:

- A participant becomes a contributor by being given **a small,
  scoped, real task** — review one plan, draft one prompt, pair with
  someone on a build. Not "would you like to contribute more?"
- A contributor becomes an ambassador by being **invited to speak**
  at a peer event, or by a colleague asking them about the chapter
  and them having a clear answer ready.
- An ambassador becomes a champion by being **offered a role** —
  track lead, facilitator — at a moment when they have time and
  appetite for it.

The captain's role is to spot moments when offering a step makes
sense. Not every participant wants to step up; not every contributor
wants to step further. Force the path and you get reluctant people
in roles. Pace it well and the right people self-select.

## Signals For Each Stage

Things to watch for that suggest someone is ready to step further.
These are signals the captain should *use*, not metrics to *optimise*.

### Signals a participant could be a contributor

- They ask thoughtful questions in the channel that reveal real
  domain expertise.
- They have attended two or more sessions without being asked.
- They reference SAAF artefacts in their other work — even if just
  in conversation.
- They have a plan idea but haven't written it down.

**Move:** invite them, by name, to write that plan. Offer to pair on
the first draft.

### Signals a contributor could be an ambassador

- They have brought a colleague to a session.
- They reference the chapter when explaining their decisions in their
  own organisation.
- A peer outside the chapter has asked them about the chapter.
- They have written something — even an internal note — about a
  chapter session.

**Move:** invite them to speak at the next IIA / ISACA / NOREA event
about a specific piece of work, or to host a fifteen-minute show-and-
tell at a peer body.

### Signals an ambassador could be a champion

- They have done the same role at multiple sessions (e.g. always
  helping facilitate the engineering track without being assigned).
- They have ideas about how the chapter could improve — and the
  ideas are operational, not abstract.
- They have asked the captain about how the chapter is run, beyond
  what they need for their own contribution.
- They are visibly trusted by other contributors.

**Move:** ask them, in private, whether they would take on a track
lead or facilitator role for the next session. Be explicit about the
time commitment and the duration. Explicit asks beat vague invitations.

## Stage Diversity Is Healthy

A healthy chapter has many participants, fewer contributors, fewer
still ambassadors, and a small number of champions. The shape:

```
                Champions          5–10
              Ambassadors       10–25
            Contributors       25–60
        Participants         50–200
```

If your chapter has the same number of champions as participants, the
chapter is too small or you have over-promoted. If your chapter has
hundreds of participants and three contributors, the contribution
mechanisms are broken.

Look at your active list once per quarter. The shape should look
roughly like the cone above, scaled to your size.

## The "Many Layers, Bounded Contribution" Principle

Contribution should be **bounded and specific**, especially for new
contributors. "Help out more" is a bad ask. "Review these two plans
before next session" is a good ask.

In SAAF, this means structuring entry points carefully:

| Entry point | Time required | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Read and respond in the channel | 5 min/week | Participant exploring the chapter |
| Review one plan | 30 min | Participant becoming a contributor |
| Pair on a build | half day | Contributor becoming a regular |
| Demo at a session | 5 min in session, ~half day prep | Contributor becoming visible |
| Host a 15-min show-and-tell | 1 hour | Contributor stepping toward ambassador |
| Track-lead a session | 2 days (incl. session) | Ambassador becoming champion |
| Facilitate a track recap | 4 hours/quarter | Champion in operating mode |
| Co-captain a chapter | 1 day/month | Senior champion / future captain |

Bounded asks let people commit without fear of unbounded obligation.
Unbounded asks ("can you help with the chapter?") get reluctant yes
and silent no.
