# When To Start (And When Not To)

Before you start a chapter, do the honest version of this exercise.

The single biggest reason internal and regional SAAF-style efforts fail
is that they are launched into conditions that cannot support them. The
launch energy gets the first session done. The next session quietly
doesn't happen.

This page exists to surface those conditions before you commit.

---

## Signals You Are Ready

Three or more of these need to be true *now*, not "soon."

**You have a named senior sponsor with skin in the game.**
For an internal chapter: a Head of Internal Audit, CIO, CRO, or equivalent
who will (a) put a quote in the launch email, (b) attend at least one
session a year, and (c) defend the budget when it is challenged.

For a regional chapter: at least one professional body (IIA, ISACA,
NOREA, equivalent) willing to endorse the chapter publicly.

**You can name three to five people who would build, not just attend.**
Not "people who would be interested." People who would block calendar
time, write a plan, host a session, or commit to bringing colleagues.
Names, not personas. If you cannot list three, you have an idea, not a
chapter.

**There is a real problem in the audit work that AI could plausibly
help with.**
Examples: closing the gap between policy and tested control, getting
out from under PBC chasing, scaling vendor risk assessments,
keeping up with regulatory change. The problem is not "AI" — the
problem is something audit work struggles with that AI could touch. If
the answer to "what problem are we solving" is "using AI," start over.

**You have a plausible venue for the first session, and a plausible host
for the second.**
A chapter that runs once is an event. A chapter that runs twice is a
chapter. The second host signals you have community lift, not just
goodwill from one organisation.

**You have time — at least one half-day per week for the first six
months.**
This is the founder time. Not the participant time. If you cannot
commit a half-day a week, find a co-captain who can, or wait.

---

## Signals You Are Not Ready

If two or more of these are true, do something else first.

**The audit team is in the middle of a major restructure.**
People in restructures do not have spare cycles, and managers will not
release them. Wait until the org chart settles.

**There is no executive who has said "yes" out loud.**
Tacit support evaporates the moment a budget question lands. If you do
not have one explicit, named executive endorsement, you have nothing.

**The motivating energy is "we should be doing more with AI."**
That phrase, by itself, is not enough fuel. It will not survive contact
with three quarters of unglamorous coordination work. You need either a
specific painful problem or a specific opportunity to translate. Vague
ambition burns out by month four.

**The plan is to "first do a pilot and see if there's interest."**
This is the most common failure pattern. The pilot is a session that
nobody owns. It produces a recap nobody reads. It teaches you nothing
about whether a real chapter would work, because a real chapter
requires multi-session commitment that a pilot cannot test.

If you must do a pilot, frame it explicitly as a *single proof event*
with a clear yes/no decision criterion at the end. Do not frame it as
"the start of a chapter we'll build if it goes well."

**You are doing this primarily to make your CV better, win business, or
launch a product.**
SAAF-style communities are detection systems for hidden agendas. They
will sniff this out within two sessions and the chapter will collapse
into vendor-pitch territory. If you have a product to sell, sponsor a
chapter someone else runs.

## Three Anti-Patterns To Avoid

These are specific shapes that look reasonable on day one and reliably
fail.

### Anti-pattern 1: The Webinar That Wishes It Was A Hackathon

You book a virtual session. Forty people register. Seven show up. The
keynote runs long. There are five minutes of "breakout discussion" and
no demo. The recap is the slide deck.

**Why it fails:** Webinars produce audiences, not communities. They
optimise for reach, not depth. SAAF works because people are in a room
with their hands on a keyboard, with someone they didn't know an hour
ago.

**Fix:** Run the first session in person, with at most fifty people,
and make sure at least 60% of the agenda is hands-on build time. If
hybrid is unavoidable, the in-room work is the centre of gravity, not
the streamed presentations.

### Anti-pattern 2: The Vendor Showcase In SAAF Clothing

A vendor offers to sponsor your kickoff. They have great catering,
great venue, great speakers. Three of the speakers are their employees.
The "build session" uses their platform. The recap features their
product.

**Why it fails:** The artefacts are not portable, the manifesto is
quietly broken, and competing vendors will not show up to session two.
You have re-skinned a vendor user group.

**Fix:** Sponsorship is welcome. Sponsorship that controls the agenda
is not. Use the [running-hackathons/speakers-and-keynotes.md](../running-hackathons/speakers-and-keynotes.md)
guidance to keep the speaker mix balanced.

### Anti-pattern 3: The Working Group That Drifted In

By session three, the afternoon hackathon has been quietly squeezed —
the morning programme runs long, lunch starts late, the build window
is half what it was. By session five, there's a steering committee.
By session seven, the output is a position paper on responsible AI in
audit. There is no working agent in the repo from the last three
sessions.

**Why it fails:** Audit is a profession that rewards careful committee
work. Without active resistance, every audit-adjacent community drifts
toward the working-group equilibrium because that is the cultural
default. SAAF is the deliberate alternative.

**Fix:** The captain's job, every session, is to push back on the
gravitational pull toward "let's discuss" and toward "let's build." If
the recap of the most recent session does not contain a demo or an
artefact, the chapter is drifting. Name it, in public, at the next
organiser meeting.

## A Twelve-Month Pre-Mortem

Before you commit, do this exercise out loud with a co-organiser:

> *"It is twelve months from today. The chapter has quietly died. Walk
> me through the most plausible story of how that happened, in three
> paragraphs."*

The most common answers are:

1. The captain left the founding organisation and nobody picked up.
2. The second host never materialised, so all sessions ran at one venue
   and the community calcified around that one organisation's politics.
3. Two senior people fell out and the room became uncomfortable.
4. Sponsorship dependency tipped the agenda and core builders left.
5. Sessions were good but plans never moved past Plan, so people
   stopped seeing the point.

For each plausible story, name the *one* thing you would do differently
*now*, before launch, to reduce the odds of that story. Write those
things into your chapter charter.

If you cannot name a single mitigation, do not launch yet. Sit on it
for a month and try again.

---

## When You Are Ready

If most of the "ready" signals are present, none of the "not ready"
signals are dominating, and you have a credible answer to the
twelve-month pre-mortem — start.

Read [internal-chapter/](../internal-chapter/) or
[regional-chapter/](../regional-chapter/) next, depending on which
shape you are starting.

Whichever shape you're starting, **the first concrete step is the
same: reach out to the main SAAF Project organisers** — Eduward van
de Kamp and Mathijs Scholten — via
[saafproject.com](https://www.saafproject.com). The conversation
saves weeks of reinventing the wheel and is the natural starting
point for SAAF Foundation affiliation, without which a chapter
cannot use the SAAF name or logo. Details for each chapter shape are
in the respective READMEs:
[internal-chapter/README.md#get-in-touch-with-the-main-saaf-project-first](../internal-chapter/README.md#get-in-touch-with-the-main-saaf-project-first)
or
[regional-chapter/README.md#get-in-touch-with-the-main-saaf-project-first](../regional-chapter/README.md#get-in-touch-with-the-main-saaf-project-first).

If you are still on the fence: run a single, deliberate proof event
using the [running-hackathons/](../running-hackathons/) playbook, with
the explicit framing "this is a one-off." Decide afterwards. That is a
better path than a half-committed soft launch.
