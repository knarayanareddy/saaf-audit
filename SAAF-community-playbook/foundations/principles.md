# Operating Principles

Ten principles for SAAF chapter founders. They extend the
[manifesto](../MANIFESTO.md) into the specific decisions you will make in
the first six months. The content is SAAF-specific; the ten-principle
shape is a deliberate echo of how comparable practitioner communities
have framed their own founding rules.

---

### 1. Start with a clear vision — and write it down.

Before you book a venue or invite a speaker, you must be able to finish
this sentence in one paragraph: *"This chapter exists to ___, for ___,
because ___."* If you cannot, your first session will be a meeting
about meetings.

The vision is not a tagline. It is a working definition of who the
community serves, what they walk away with, and what they bring back.
Pin it to the chapter charter ([template](../appendix/templates/chapter-charter.md)).

---

### 2. Secure executive air cover before recruiting champions.

For an internal chapter: get the Head of Internal Audit and at least one
other senior sponsor (CIO, CRO, CPO) to commit time, budget, and
public endorsement *before* you ask anyone to volunteer. Champions who
join without air cover get punished by their line managers for
"distraction."

For a regional chapter: get at least one professional body (IIA, ISACA,
NOREA, equivalent) to endorse the chapter. The endorsement is not money;
it is permission for members of that body to spend their CPE / CPD time
on the work.

---

### 3. Nominate a dedicated captain.

Every chapter needs one named person whose explicit job is to keep the
chapter alive. Co-organisers are fine. Anonymous collective ownership
is not. When the captain rotates, the rotation is announced, and the
new captain is named publicly.

Captain duties: book sessions, write the recap, defend the manifesto,
escalate when the chapter drifts, hand over cleanly.

---

### 4. Trust your champions.

A champion is not a delegate. Once someone has earned a champion role
(see the [advocacy funnel](../engagement-and-growth/advocacy-funnel.md)),
trust them with real work: facilitating a track, reviewing plans,
mentoring a newcomer. Do not turn champions into messengers.

If you cannot trust the people you have recruited, you have recruited
the wrong people, or recruited too few.

---

### 5. Build community, not audience.

Communities have peer-to-peer conversations. Audiences receive
broadcasts. Most chapters that fail do so by drifting into broadcast
mode — newsletters, webinars, slide decks — and losing the
participatory rhythm that makes SAAF work.

The cheap test: in the last month, did members talk to *each other*
more than they talked to the captain? If not, you have an audience.

---

### 6. Promote knowledge sharing — by default and on rails.

Every artefact ships back to the community by default. The default is
share; the exception is withhold; the exception needs a reason. Build
"share-back" into the *workflow* — the plan template, the recap, the
PR review — not into people's good intentions. Good intentions don't
scale.

The pattern across community work is consistent: contributions that
are *visible and connected to outcomes* are the ones that keep coming.
Make the share rail the path of least resistance.

---

### 7. Reward responsibility, recognise contribution.

Champions take on real work, often unpaid. They are repaid in three
currencies: visibility, learning, and access.

- **Visibility:** their name on the artefact, in the recap, on the
  leaderboard.
- **Learning:** first access to new tooling, first invite to advanced
  sessions, mentorship from more senior community members.
- **Access:** to the captain, to sponsors, to the main SAAF Project
  organisers.

What you must avoid: turning recognition into a points-and-prizes
gimmick. Audit professionals are adults. Recognise them as adults.

---

### 8. Invest in your champions.

Budget, even for an internal chapter, must include a line item for
champion development: training, conference travel, paid time for plan
contributions. If the budget is zero, the chapter is signalling that
the work is hobby-grade. People notice.

If you have no money at all: invest *time*. Block calendars. Protect
champions from line-manager interruptions during session days.
Negotiate with their managers in advance, not after.

---

### 9. Anticipate personnel changes.

People move. Champions get promoted, change jobs, take parental leave,
go to a competitor. A chapter that depends on three specific people is
one bad quarter away from extinction.

Defences against personnel risk:

- A captain succession plan (named understudy).
- Plans owned by named individuals, but with a `Claimed By` field and
  a fallback claimant.
- Recaps written within one week of every session, while memory is
  fresh, by someone other than the captain when possible.
- Onboarding rituals that assume the people running them are not the
  founders.

---

### 10. Defend the spirit, in public.

When sponsorship pressure, vendor capture, scope creep, or a single
loud voice threatens what the chapter is, name it in the room. Quietly
disagreeing in the corridor does not work; the manifesto cannot defend
itself.

This is the principle most chapter founders skip, and it is the one
most often quoted by founders of *failed* chapters when they reflect
on what they wish they had done differently.

---

## How To Use These Principles

These are not aspirations. They are how the chapter operates on its
worst day, when something has gone wrong. Print them, read them at the
start of organiser meetings, and use them as the lens for retros.

A chapter that breaks one principle has a problem to fix. A chapter
that breaks three is not a SAAF chapter anymore.
