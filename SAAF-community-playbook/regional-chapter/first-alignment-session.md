# The First Alignment Session

The chapter's first session is not a hackathon. It is an **alignment
session**. It exists to put the manifesto, the four pillars, the
roles, and the cadence in front of the room — and to let the room
push back, ask questions, and commit (or not).

The main SAAF Project ran an alignment session before its first
hackathon. Most regional chapters benefit from doing the same. If you
skip this and go straight to a build day, the first build day is
half-empty of intent.

---

## The Shape Of An Alignment Session

A half-day, in-person, with thirty to fifty people. Three blocks:

| Block | Duration | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Welcome and framing | 60 min | Captain + sponsor + endorsing body. The why, the manifesto, the shape of the chapter. |
| Working groups | 90 min | Small-group discussion of dilemmas, expectations, role allocation. |
| Closing and commitment | 30 min | Each working group reports back. Captain commits to next session date and venue. |

Then drinks, with no formal programme.

The session does not produce a working agent. It produces:

- A room that has met each other.
- A shared understanding of what the chapter is.
- A list of plan ideas to develop before the next session.
- Names of people who have committed to specific roles.

---

## The Welcome And Framing Block

Sixty minutes. Three short presentations, never more than twenty
minutes each:

### Twenty minutes — The captain

What this chapter is, what it is not. The vision paragraph from the
charter. The manifesto, on a slide, read aloud. The federation with
the main SAAF Project. The cadence (quarterly hackathons starting
in [date]).

Speak to the dilemmas the room cares about: how do we share without
breaching client confidentiality, how do we work with vendors without
being captured by them, how do we credit contributors fairly. Quote
the manifesto's answers.

### Twenty minutes — A senior sponsor or endorsing body

Someone with weight in the profession, who can say *"this is good for
audit, and this is the kind of work members of [our profession] should
be doing."* They are not selling — they are giving the room
permission to take the chapter seriously.

This is a structural slot, not an honour. The wrong person here can
sour the room (a vendor giving a sales pitch, a senior who treats the
chapter as a side project to their own initiative). The right person
sets up the rest of the day.

### Twenty minutes — A live demo from the founding team

Someone — usually the builder shape from the founding team — shows a
working agent on synthetic data. Five minutes of demo, fifteen of
"how I built it" and "what was hard." The room sees what the
chapter's output looks like. They have something concrete to react to
in the working groups.

Avoid keynote speakers from outside the founding team for the first
alignment session. The first session needs to feel like *the room's*
session, not a conference.

---

## The Working Groups Block

Ninety minutes, four to six groups of six to eight people, each
discussing a dilemma. The dilemmas are deliberately framed as
*choices*, not lectures.

Sample dilemmas (adapt to your chapter):

- **The First Step Dilemma.** Do we go broad and try to cover every
  audit phase, or pick one phase and go deep?
- **The Human Expertise Dilemma.** Are we building agents that
  replace audit work, or agents that assist auditors? What's the
  default assumption?
- **The Sharing Dilemma.** What does "share back to the community"
  mean when our prompts encode our internal control framework?
- **The Vendor Dilemma.** Two vendors are at this session. They want
  to sponsor. What conditions do we attach?
- **The Cadence Dilemma.** Quarterly is a lot of organising. Bi-annual
  is too sparse to maintain momentum. What's the right rhythm for our
  geography and our member organisations?

Each group has a facilitator (usually a founding-team member). Each
group produces a one-page summary: where the group landed, where it
disagreed, and what the group thinks the chapter should do.

The dilemmas matter less than the conversation. The room comes out
of working groups having heard each other's reasoning, which is the
foundation for everything else.

---

## The Closing And Commitment Block

Thirty minutes. Each working group reports back in three to five
minutes. The captain notes the points where groups converged and
where they did not.

Then the captain commits, in front of the room, to:

- The date and venue of the first hackathon.
- The format of the first hackathon (refer to
  [running-hackathons/format-and-agenda.md](../running-hackathons/format-and-agenda.md)).
- The deadline for plan submissions before the first hackathon.
- A specific call to action: *"By [date], we want N plans submitted.
  If you have an idea, find me or [co-captain] before the drinks
  end."*

If the captain cannot commit to specific dates and a specific call to
action by the closing block, the alignment session has failed in its
purpose. Reschedule the chapter's launch.

---

## The Drinks

Drinks are a working session, even though they look social. The
captain and the founding team should be circulating, not clustered.
Specific things to do during drinks:

- Get the names and emails of attendees who said something useful in
  working groups.
- Nudge two or three people who stayed quiet during groups but seemed
  engaged — the next champions are often there.
- Confirm with founding partners that their organisation will send
  builders to the first hackathon.
- Talk to vendors who attended about how they can contribute *without*
  being the headline.

Do not let the drinks become a pitch session for vendors. The captain
or co-captain should physically be near any vendor conversation that
is escalating in that direction.

---

## After The Alignment Session

Within seven days:

- Send a thank-you-and-recap email to all attendees. Include the
  manifesto, the date and venue of the first hackathon, the plan
  submission deadline, and a link to the chapter charter.
- Update the chapter's repo / website with the alignment session
  recap.
- Write personal one-line notes to the working group facilitators and
  the demo presenter. They held the day together; thank them
  specifically.

Within fourteen days:

- One-on-one meetings with the three to five strongest contributors
  from the alignment session. Recruit the first wave of champions.
- Begin reviewing plan submissions as they arrive.

Within thirty days:

- Send the first plan-status update to the chapter's mailing list:
  *"X plans submitted, Y are likely candidates for the first
  hackathon, here are the gaps we'd love to see filled."*

The first hackathon should land roughly eight to twelve weeks after
the alignment session. Sooner and people feel rushed. Later and the
energy from the alignment session has dissipated.

---

## What Could Go Wrong

**Low attendance.** If only fifteen people show up, the working
groups become awkward and the room feels small. *Mitigation:* over-
invite by 50% (registration drop-off is consistent), and have the
founding team book personal one-on-ones with at least twenty senior
contacts in advance to confirm attendance.

**A dilemma group goes off the rails.** One person dominates, or the
group can't agree on anything. *Mitigation:* facilitators are
briefed in advance to redirect; rotate quiet participants to speak;
end the dilemma at the time limit even if unresolved — unresolved is
a fine outcome, the chapter does not need consensus on day one.

**The sponsor or endorsing body speaker goes long.** Twenty minutes
becomes thirty-five, and the day collapses backward. *Mitigation:*
brief them in advance with a hard time limit and a backup signal
("when I raise my hand it means three minutes"). Do not be afraid to
cut them off. The room expects you to.

**A vendor monopolises the room.** They have hosted, they sponsored
the catering, and now they are working the crowd. *Mitigation:* the
captain is in the room, visible, and gently breaks up monopolised
conversations. Refer to
[identity-and-positioning.md](identity-and-positioning.md) for how to
manage vendor expectations before the day.
