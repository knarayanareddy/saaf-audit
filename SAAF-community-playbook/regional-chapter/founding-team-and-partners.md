# Founding Team And Partners

You cannot start a regional chapter alone. You can start one with two
or three people. The shape of those first few people determines almost
everything.

This page covers the founding team (humans) and the partner
organisations (institutions). Both have to be in place before the
first session.

---

## The Founding Team

Two to four people. Each must bring something the others do not. The
ideal founding team has the following four shapes covered, even if
some shapes share a body:

### Shape 1 — The convener

Someone with the network. They know enough auditors across enough
organisations to fill the first session by personal invitation. They
return calls. They are trusted across competing firms — usually
because they have worked at more than one of them, or have a long
career in the profession that puts them above the firm-rivalry layer.

This is often a former Big Four partner who has moved in-house, a
senior NOREA / IIA member, an academic who teaches across the
profession, or an ex-regulator. Without a convener, the chapter
struggles to fill its first session.

### Shape 2 — The builder

Someone who can stand at a whiteboard with auditors and engineers in
the same room and make working software happen. They do not have to be
the best engineer in the room — they have to be the most *unintimidating*
engineer in the room. They translate audit problems into agent
problems and back.

The builder is often the first person to demo something at session
zero. Their visible, unpolished demo on day one signals to the room
"this is what we do here."

### Shape 3 — The professional-body bridge

Someone with credibility inside one or more endorsing professional
bodies — IIA, ISACA, NOREA, NBA, ForHumanity, equivalent. They know
the certification cycle, the CPE rules, the publication channels. They
get the endorsement letter signed. They get the chapter mentioned in
the body's newsletter.

Without this shape, the chapter is harder to legitimise across
organisations. Internal champions inside member organisations need a
defensible answer when their compliance team asks "what is this and
why are we sending people to it."

### Shape 4 — The operator

Someone whose pleasure in life is making logistics work. Venue, dates,
catering, the registration form, the recap. They are not invisible;
they are the reason the day works. Communities that lack an operator
collapse not because of bad ideas but because session four never
happens.

The captain might be the operator, or the co-captain, or a separate
fourth person. What matters is that there is a name on the operations.

### Where the captain comes from

The captain can be the convener, the builder, or the bridge — rarely
the operator (the operator role is too time-consuming to also hold the
captain role). In practice, in most regional chapters, the captain has
been the convener, because the captain's job in year one is mostly
convening.

The remaining shapes go to co-captains, founding partners, and early
champions.

## Partner Organisations

A partner is a member organisation that has formally committed to the
chapter. Two to four founding partners, then open membership.

### What a founding partner commits to

| Commitment | Detail |
|---|---|
| Attendance | At least one builder at each of the first four sessions |
| Hosting | Host at least one session in the first 18 months |
| Contribution | At least one plan submitted in the first year |
| Public endorsement | Logo on the chapter's site (if the chapter has one); a quote from a senior leader for the launch announcement |

Founding partners are *not* sponsors and do not pay a fee. They are
member organisations that have skin in the game and visible commitment.

### How to recruit founding partners

The order matters. Start with the easiest commitments and escalate.

1. **Initial conversation with the audit lead.** Twenty minutes,
   one-on-one, no slide deck. Explain the chapter, the manifesto, the
   federation with the main SAAF Project. Ask: *"Would your
   organisation send one builder to a quarterly session?"*

2. **Manager-brief follow-up.** Send the
   [manager brief](../appendix/templates/manager-brief.md) — what the
   chapter is, what the time commitment is, what the organisation
   gets in return. The audit lead forwards it to the relevant
   manager(s).

3. **First-builder confirmation.** When the first builder is named,
   send a welcome note that includes the manifesto and the upcoming
   session details. Cc the audit lead.

4. **Founding-partner formalisation.** Once a builder has attended a
   session and the audit lead has expressed interest in continuing,
   ask explicitly: *"Would you be a founding partner?"* Frame what
   that means in writing. Get a "yes" in email.

Do not skip the steps. Skipping to "founding partner" before the
audit lead has seen what the chapter actually is leads to soft yes,
hard backoff six months later.

### Mix of partners

Aim for variety in the founding partner mix:

- **Diverse organisation types.** Not all Big Four firms. Mix in a
  bank, an industrial group, a regulator, a public-sector body. The
  diversity of audit problems is what makes the chapter interesting.
- **Geographic diversity inside the region.** If the chapter is "SAAF
  DACH," do not let the founding partners all be in Frankfurt. Mix
  Frankfurt, Munich, Vienna, Zurich.
- **No more than one partner from a single market segment.** Two Big
  Four firms is fine. Three creates a cohort that will dominate the
  conversation.

If the founding partner mix is monolithic, the chapter's discussions
will be too — and other organisations will feel like outsiders.

## Endorsing Professional Bodies

The endorsement is the single most leveraged item in the chapter's
launch. Members of the endorsing body have a defensible reason to
spend CPE / CPD time on the chapter. Their managers can point at the
endorsement when justifying the time. The chapter can quote the
endorsement in its launch announcement.

Endorsements are slow — plan for them to take three to six months.
Start the conversation early.

What an endorsement letter typically contains:

- Recognition of the chapter as a legitimate professional development
  forum for the body's members.
- A statement that participation can count toward CPE / CPD hours.
- A commitment to mention the chapter in the body's communications
  channels.
- An annual reaffirmation clause.

What it does *not* contain:

- Approval rights over the chapter's agenda.
- A requirement to use the body's branding or governance structures.
- A funding obligation either way.

If the body insists on agenda approval rights, decline the endorsement.
The chapter is better off with no endorsement than with an endorsement
that compromises neutrality.

## Vendors

Vendors are not founding partners. Vendors can be:

- **Sponsors** — contributing money, venue, or in-kind support without
  any agenda or branding control.
- **Participants** — sending engineers and product people to attend
  and contribute, like any member organisation.
- **Speakers** — giving short, non-product talks on relevant topics
  (for example, a vendor's research team presenting on agent
  evaluation methodology).

Vendors are *not*:

- Founding partners.
- Captains or co-captains.
- The exclusive sponsor of any session.
- Permitted to demo their own product as the headline of a session.

The line is sometimes uncomfortable to hold. Some vendors are full of
genuinely helpful people who have been excellent contributors. The
line is structural, not personal — it protects the chapter's
neutrality, which is what makes the room across-firewall safe.

See [identity-and-positioning.md](identity-and-positioning.md) for
how to communicate the vendor relationship publicly.

## A Founding-Team Health Check

Three months before launch, ask:

1. *Do we have all four shapes (convener, builder, bridge, operator)
   covered?* If not, find the missing person before booking a venue.
2. *Do we have at least two founding partners with signed-up
   builders?* If not, the launch is premature.
3. *Do we have one endorsing body who has agreed in writing?* If not,
   delay launch until the letter arrives, even if everything else is
   ready.

If any of these is "no," and you launch anyway, you are betting that
you will resolve them in flight. Sometimes that bet pays off. More
often, the missing piece becomes the reason the chapter stalls in
month nine.
