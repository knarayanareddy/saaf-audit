# Building Internal Alignment

> Translating community work into language executives understand —
> *before* the budget conversation forces you to.

The hardest skill of an internal chapter captain is not facilitation.
It is translation. Specifically: turning what happened at the last
hackathon into terms that change what your CFO, your CIO, and your CIA
do *next*.

This page is about that translation. The framework below is the
standard community-to-executive translation pattern, adapted for an
audit-organisation context.

---

## The Core Mistake

Most internal chapters describe themselves to executives like this:

> *"We had 38 people from across audit, risk, and IT join our quarterly
> hackathon. They built 9 prototype agents covering vendor risk,
> evidence extraction, and policy-to-control mapping. Engagement was
> high; participants gave the day a 4.6/5 rating."*

That paragraph contains an audience metric, an output count, and a
satisfaction score. It contains zero things the executive can decide
about. It is **activity reporting**, not **outcome reporting** — the
distinction that separates community work executives notice from
community work executives fund.

The same chapter, translated:

> *"Two of the agents built last quarter — vendor-risk pre-screen and
> evidence-extraction — are now in private adoption inside Audit Team
> A. Vendor risk pre-screening time has dropped from 4 hours to 30
> minutes per supplier on the synthetic test set; we are validating
> against live data this quarter. The policy-to-control work surfaced
> three control gaps in our existing ISO 27001 mapping that we did not
> previously have. Recommend: extend pilot to Audit Team B; budget
> €15k for Professional Practices validation hours."*

Same event. Different conversation.

---

## What Executives Actually Want To Hear

An effective executive update answers three questions:

1. **Which decisions did community work clarify earlier than they would
   have been otherwise?**
2. **What risks or opportunities surfaced first through the community
   that would otherwise have been late?**
3. **Where did community context reduce internal debate or rework?**

In an audit context, the SAAF translations of those questions are:

| Generic question | SAAF translation |
|---|---|
| Decisions clarified earlier | "We piloted Agent X on synthetic data and decided to / not to invest in it for live audit work." |
| Risks surfaced first | "The policy-to-control mapping found three gaps in our ISO 27001 control universe; the audit plan now covers them." |
| Internal debate reduced | "The audit-quality team and the engineering team agreed on a finding schema that ends three years of disagreement about how AI-generated findings get reviewed." |

If your update cannot answer at least one of those three, you are
reporting activity, not value.

## The Four Value Pillars (For Internal Chapters)

A four-pillar approach to community value, applied to internal SAAF
chapters:

### Pillar 1 — Audit hours returned

How much hands-on auditor time has the chapter freed, against what
baseline. Measured per audit task, on synthetic or anonymised data,
with a stated assumption about how it scales to live.

Example: *"Evidence-extraction agent reduced PBC review time from 90
min/document to 25 min/document on a 30-document synthetic test set.
At our current PBC volume of ~400/year, that is roughly 430 hours of
senior auditor time, *if* live performance matches synthetic. Live
validation in Q3."*

### Pillar 2 — Coverage and confidence

Where the chapter has measurably extended audit coverage or reduced
sample-based blind spots. AI agents that test 100% of a population
instead of a 25-item sample do not save time; they expand assurance.

Example: *"Synthetic access-review agent reviewed all 200 records in
the test population vs. our prior 25-item sample. Three exceptions
that would have been missed by sampling were detected. Methodology
team is updating the sampling guidance."*

### Pillar 3 — Risk surfaced earlier

Findings, gaps, or regulatory mismatches that the chapter surfaced
earlier than the normal audit cycle would have. This is the highest-
value pillar in the executive's eyes — it is the closest analogue to
the audit function's stated purpose.

Example: *"Regulatory change tracker flagged a draft DNB circular six
weeks before our standard quarterly review would have surfaced it.
Three control owners had time to update before the publication date."*

### Pillar 4 — Capability and resilience

The chapter is producing people, not just artefacts. How many auditors
can now build, prompt, or critically review an AI-assisted output that
could not last year? This pillar matures slowly and shows up in
recruitment, retention, and audit-plan ambition.

Example: *"Twelve auditors completed the 'review an AI-generated
finding' walkthrough this quarter, up from three last quarter. Audit
plan for next year includes two engagements that we previously would
have outsourced for lack of internal capacity."*

## How To Translate Without Lying

The temptation, when an executive asks for ROI, is to over-promise:
extrapolate synthetic-data results to live, count saved hours that
weren't really saved, claim risks the agent didn't really surface.

This is the fastest way to lose executive trust permanently. Auditors
of all people should know better.

The discipline:

- **State the population.** "On a 30-document synthetic test set" is
  not the same as "across our annual PBC volume." Be explicit.
- **State the assumption.** "If live performance matches synthetic" is
  the difference between an honest projection and a fabrication.
- **State what you don't know.** "We have not yet validated against
  live data" is a sign of seriousness, not weakness.
- **Use directional language.** "Roughly," "on the order of," "we
  expect" beat false precision. Executives do not need perfect
  attribution; they need confidence that community work contributes
  to outcomes they already manage.

## Reporting Cadence

For an internal chapter, three reporting beats matter:

**The five-line update (monthly).**
A short email to the executive sponsor: what shipped, what the
chapter is working on, what is blocked, what decision the executive
needs to make this month, when the next session is. Five lines, one
per item.

**The quarterly readout (one page).**
Structured against the four value pillars above. Not a slide deck.
One page. Read in five minutes. Decisions, not activity.

**The annual case (three pages).**
The case for the next year's budget. Includes an honest "what we got
wrong" section. Executives who see "what we got wrong" believe the
"what we got right" sections more.

---

## The Pre-Read Discipline

Before any executive update, run the draft past one champion who is
*not* a chapter founder. Ask one question: *"Does this say something
the sponsor can act on, or is it a status update?"*

If the champion can't find the action, rewrite. Status updates do not
keep budgets. Decisions do.

---

## When The Sponsor Changes

This will happen. Sponsors get promoted, leave, or move portfolios.
The chapter survives this if, before the change:

- The next sponsor candidate has been to at least one session.
- They have read the most recent quarterly readout and found it useful.
- The captain has had at least one one-on-one with them about the
  chapter.

If none of those have happened, the new sponsor will inherit the
chapter as a line item they did not choose. They will ask hard
questions. The chapter must already have answers.
