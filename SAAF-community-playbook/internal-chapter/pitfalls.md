# Internal-Chapter Pitfalls

Five failure modes specific to internal chapters, with how to recognise
them early and what to do about each.

These are observed patterns. If you find yourself in one, the playbook
recommends the move; the chapter chooses whether to take it.

---

## 1. The chapter becomes a tool-evaluation committee

**What it looks like.** Sessions start being about which platform to
buy. Demos are demos *of vendors*. Plans become RFP-shaped documents.
The "build" component shrinks until it is gone.

**Why it happens.** Procurement teams notice the chapter has the right
people in the room, and ask it to evaluate a tool "on the side." It
seems efficient. It is.

**Why it's a failure.** A vendor-evaluation committee is a different
animal — it has different governance, different conflicts of interest,
and different success criteria. SAAF cannot do both at once. Trying to
will hollow the chapter out.

**Recognise it.** If, in the last three sessions, more demo time was
spent on vendor products than on artefacts the room built, you are
already drifting.

**What to do.** Spin the procurement work out into a separate effort,
explicitly. Keep the chapter's job clear. Write it into the next
quarterly readout: *"The chapter does not do vendor evaluation; that
work has moved to [X]."* Be public about it.

---

## 2. The disengaged champion who stays loud

**What it looks like.** A founding champion has stopped contributing
real work but still attends every meeting, still has opinions, still
takes up time in the room. New champions hesitate to challenge them
because they have history.

**Why it happens.** Communities are bad at exits. Audit organisations
are particularly bad — there is a cultural reluctance to "demote"
anyone, even informally.

**Why it's a failure.** The signal sent to new champions is that
seniority in the chapter is permanent and based on tenure rather than
contribution. New champions disengage in turn. Within two quarters,
you have a chapter run by people who don't build anything.

**Recognise it.** When a current champion's name does not appear on
any plan, recap, or session in the last six months *and* their
contributions in conversation are mostly vetoes rather than ideas, the
pattern is in motion.

**What to do.** Give the champion a graceful off-ramp:

- A one-on-one acknowledging their contribution and asking what they
  want from the chapter going forward.
- A specific, scoped role they can opt into ("plan reviewer for
  controls-mapping plans, four hours per quarter") *or* an explicit
  "alumnus" status with no expectation of ongoing work.
- Public thanks, in the next recap, framed as completion not exit.

If they refuse a graceful exit and continue to block work, the captain
must escalate to the sponsor. This is what sponsors are for.

## 3. The "we'll productionise it later" graveyard

**What it looks like.** Each session produces working demos. The demos
are good. None of them ever land in production. The same auditor
shows the same near-finished agent at three consecutive sessions.

**Why it happens.** The Do stage of PDCA is fun. The Check and Act
stages are unglamorous: integration, testing, Professional Practices review,
deployment. Without explicit ownership, those stages are nobody's job.

**Why it's a failure.** Builders stop building. They notice their work
never lands and conclude — correctly — that the chapter is not a
serious vehicle for impact. They drift to other projects.

**Recognise it.** Look at the plans portfolio. How many plans are at
Plan stage vs Do vs Check vs Act? In a healthy internal chapter, you
should see a stream of plans completing Act each quarter. If Plan and
Do stages are growing while Check and Act are flat, you have the
problem.

**What to do.** Designate explicit Check and Act ownership. The
production-readiness work needs a named owner per plan, with a budget
of Professional Practices hours and a target audit engagement. The captain's
quarterly readout should report Check and Act counts, not just Plan
and Do.

If the executive sponsor cannot allocate Professional Practices hours and a pilot
team, the chapter is currently a sandbox by design — and that is OK,
but it must be named that way explicitly to participants. Hidden
sandboxes are demoralising.

## 4. The chapter that captures one team and stops there

**What it looks like.** All the active champions are from the same
audit team or the same office. Sessions feel like internal team
meetings of that team. Other audit teams stop attending. The chapter
becomes "Team A's AI side project."

**Why it happens.** Every chapter starts somewhere. The founding team
is usually clustered. Without deliberate counter-effort, the chapter
will calcify around them — they know each other's working styles,
they respond to each other's messages quickly, new members feel like
outsiders.

**Why it's a failure.** An internal chapter exists to spread audit-AI
capability across the organisation. A team-captured chapter does not
do that. Worse, it creates a perception inside the organisation that
"AI in audit" belongs to one team, which makes it harder for other
teams to start their own work.

**Recognise it.** Look at the last three sessions' attendee lists. If
more than 60% come from one team, or one office, or one reporting
line, you are captured.

**What to do.** Run the next session at a different team's office, with
a co-host from that team. Recruit at least three champions from
outside the founding team within the next two quarters. Write the
quarterly readout in a way that highlights cross-team contribution.

If you cannot recruit cross-team champions, the chapter has a deeper
problem with its sponsor. The sponsor's mandate must explicitly cover
all audit teams, or the chapter will stay captured.

---

## 5. The slow-motion vendor capture

**What it looks like.** A vendor is the host of multiple sessions. The
chapter's chosen stack is the vendor's stack. New champions are told
"we use [Vendor] here." The chapter's recaps are quietly co-marketed.

**Why it happens.** Vendors offer real things — venues, lunches,
engineering support, sometimes a generous discount on their tooling.
Captains under-resourced and under-budgeted accept these things one at
a time, and each acceptance is reasonable. The cumulative effect is
not.

**Why it's a failure.** The chapter has stopped being vendor-agnostic.
Cross-vendor participants leave. The chapter's artefacts are no
longer portable. When the vendor relationship sours (acquisition,
pricing change, contract dispute), the chapter dies with it.

**Recognise it.** If a participant new to the chapter could not run
the most recent demo without a license to a specific commercial
product, you are captured. If three or more sessions in a row used the
same vendor's venue, you are at high risk.

**What to do.** Re-read the [manifesto](../MANIFESTO.md) section on
vendor neutrality. Then rotate the next two sessions' venues to
non-vendor hosts (a member organisation, a professional body, a
university). Audit the chapter's stack: which artefacts depend on a
specific vendor's product, and what would the equivalent look like in
an open or alternative stack?

The decision is not always "leave the vendor stack." Sometimes the
right call is "pick this vendor for these reasons, document the
choice, and revisit annually." But that decision must be made
explicitly and in the open, not by drift.

## A General Diagnostic

Once a quarter, ask the following five questions in an organiser
meeting:

1. *Is anyone disengaging? Why?*
2. *Are plans completing, or just accumulating?*
3. *Who is missing from the room that should be there?*
4. *Is any single voice — a champion, a sponsor, a vendor — taking
   more space than the manifesto allows?*
5. *If we shut this down tomorrow, what would the organisation lose?*

The fifth question is the most useful. If the answer is "very little,"
you have a chapter that is performing community work without producing
community value. Re-read [foundations/](../foundations/) and
[../measuring-value/](../measuring-value/), and decide whether to
rebuild or retire.
